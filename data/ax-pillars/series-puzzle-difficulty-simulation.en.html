What this series found

  • Block Turn's spec promised that all three pieces in a tray could always be placed. Under that rule a planning bot played 80 games and all 80 reached the 3,000-move cap. We compared seven supply-rule versions with two bots, chose v7, and now keep the promise for the first 10 trays only. (Part 1: simulation bots)
  • All 1,100 Arrow Knots levels come from a reverse-construction generator built on a move rule fixed first, so every board is solvable as built. One generator fix cut the share of arrows removable on the first move in the hardest band from 0.50 to 0.11. (Part 2: the level generator)
  • Testers said the timer was too generous. A 158-level simulation set a 30% cut; the first proposal, 45%, left casual players a 1% three-star rate past level 500. (Part 3: time limits)
  • Seven of 16 ad-acquired devices stalled on board 4 without a single solve. Solving the first 10 boards exactly with a player model found four walls, but board 4 was not one of them, so the data could not say why those seven left. (Part 4: first-chapter walls)
  • We slowed a spelling mini game twice. Replaying the first slowdown in a player model, beginners still lost a heart in the first minute in 100% of runs: the block was recalling the right word, not speed. (Part 5: two speed fixes that failed)

This series is for solo developers and small teams making puzzle games. If you plan to generate levels, start with Part 2; if your game is live and day-one churn is high, start with Part 4. Parts 1 and 3 use the same idea, re-scoring one set of bot runs or play logs against each candidate rule, so they read well together. Part 5 is the failure case where simulation came only after complaints; read it the day someone tells you the game is too hard.
