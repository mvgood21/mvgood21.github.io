App privacy policy templates: we threw ours out and rewrote it from a code audit

Four apps shared one generated privacy policy. We replaced it with 8 pages written from source, each checked by a second agent looking for counter-evidence.

By SugarMount · Website and search · 11 min read · Last updated November 7, 2026

Four of our Android apps pointed at one English template that said user information "is not collected by me in any way", while one of them, the Korean spell checker SugarSpeller 1.6.025, was sending AI explanation requests and an anonymous ID to our own server. On October 4, 2026, 8 agents read the four codebases for 11 min 46 s and wrote 8 Korean and English policy pages, published at 11:30 in commit d75409b. We treat a privacy policy as a code audit report first and a legal document second, so the site's rules now say that a fact you cannot verify gets left out.

Sources: git history of this site and the app repositories, work session records from September 29 to October 4 and October 17, 2026, and SugarSpeller's release runbook and monitoring log. Commit counts and versions were re-measured on November 4, 2026. This note is not legal review, and it does not judge whether our policies meet any statute.

Where the template and the 1.6.025 code disagreed

The template still ended with "created at privacypolicytemplate.net and modified/generated by App Privacy Policy Generator". It opened with a line ending "built the This app as a Free app." The word This sat where the app name belonged.

TemplateSugarSpeller 1.6.025 code
"retained on your device and is not collected by me in any way"AI explanation requests (corrected words up to 60 characters each, error type, checker, subscription status, app version) and a Firebase anonymous ID reach our server.
Two third parties: Google Play Services, AdMobNaver and Daum (Korean portals whose spell checkers do the checking), Firebase, Vertex AI (Gemini) and the ad partner Liftoff also receive data.
"Free app", "at no cost"Sells Google Play subscriptions.

1.6.025 was the first version to send data to our own server, but text to be checked had gone to Naver and Daum for longer than that, so the third-party list was already short. The design of the AI feature is in the first LLM in our spell checker.

We wrote down that it was wrong, and it stayed until October 4

At 21:49 on September 29, commit 0b64ea6 in the SugarSpeller repository added draft policy text and a Play Data safety answer table. The draft states that the published policy "is the template as is" and does not match reality. Step 3 of the release runbook says to fix the policy and the Data safety form before submitting the app.

When an agent fetched the Google Sites page at 11:05 on October 4, the paragraph was not there. 1.6.025 was rolling out by the night of October 1, so that version ran under the wrong policy for about two and a half days. The monitoring log counted 254 users on 1.6.025 between September 22 and October 3, with no note that test devices were excluded.

The agent's release notes on the night of September 29 said the policy had to change before submission, and on September 30 it handed the item back as the owner's third remaining task. Nothing records why the item stayed open, but the structure that allowed it is visible.

  1. The policy's sentences had no source in the code. A generator wrote them, so nothing could flag a mismatch when the code moved.
  2. The policy lived on Google Sites, outside any repository. Fixing it was one manual line in a runbook, and the four app repositories have no .github/workflows (checked November 4).
  3. Four apps shared one URL, as their Play listings showed. There was no page on which to state a fact about one app.

The one-paragraph plan rested on a sentence that did not exist

The September 29 plan kept the template and added a paragraph under "Information Collection and Use". The agent-written draft says the analytics and ads section could keep its "existing text (Google Analytics for Firebase, AdMob)". Searching the template for Analytics returns zero hits.

The paragraph would not have been enough anyway. On a shared page, a sentence about sending text to Naver would also have landed in the policy of SugarAlbum, a video maker, and "Free app" would have stayed.

The owner's request on October 4 was to move policies and app information to one site. Moving alone would have carried the same sentence to a new address. The audit was the agent's addition, and so was its size: 8 agents and about 1 million tokens excluding cache reads, which the agent reported as not requested.

One writer per app, one reviewer hunting counter-evidence

Four writer agents were told to "only claim what the code does" and, when unsure, to "disclose rather than deny". Four other agents then took the files, looked for evidence against every "we don't collect or send X" sentence, and fixed what was wrong. All four apps needed fixes.

AppWriter's draftAfter review
SugarSpellerSends words, error type and checker name to Vertex AIWords and error type only. The server passes original, corrected and type.
SugarCardNo ads while subscribedThe basic plan keeps the banner, and the exit-screen ad shows on every plan.
SugarAlbumThree groups of permissions missingAdded storage write, ad API and Firebase permissions after reading the merged release manifest.
SugarClipAndroid erases it beyond recoveryRemoved with app data or the app; backup copies are deleted in the Google account.

The first row claimed too much and the second flattered the app. These agents had the source open and the "only claim what the code does" instruction in front of them, and they still wrote those sentences. The workflow script had split writers from reviewers before any draft existed.

Reviewers also settled values that live outside the code. The SugarSpeller writer flagged that "deleted after 30 days" depended on a Firestore TTL policy it could not confirm. The reviewer read it from the API (ttlConfig.state: ACTIVE) and read retentionDays: 30 from the log bucket. That TTL was step 1 of the same runbook, also manual: a query at 16:00 on October 3 found it unset, and one at 01:00 on October 4 found it ACTIVE.

The numbers we deleted and the claim the owner dropped

On October 5, bdbbe97 put "Policies describe the actual code. Don't add claims the code doesn't back." into the site's agent instructions file, AGENTS.md. On October 18, b82faac added "If a fact cannot be verified there, leave it out."

Commit 13d00b8 in our family calendar app AXFamily (October 10) changed the invite link default from 72 hours to the 24 in the code, replaced "an internal ID instead of a name" with the fact that display names are sent, and deleted two numbers found nowhere in code or repository: "integrity tokens 12 hours" and "Play grace period 7 days". The same commit's move to the Seoul region turned out wrong, a story told in the AI processing notice we changed twice in a day.

On November 1 a person made the call. The Block Turn page promised no banner or interstitial in the first three games, but a classic game over counts as two runs, so an interstitial could appear from the second game over. The owner kept the code and removed the sentence, and commit 6ab2edb records it as an owner decision.

Policy work kept coming. After d75409b, through November 4, 22 of 104 commits have privacy, policy or the Korean word for it in the subject line, mostly policies for new apps. Across the whole repository it is 29 of 117. That is a subject-line grep, so one AX notes commit is in there by accident.

The same defect in a Play Data safety form on October 17

One more place states the same facts. At 14:41 on October 17, the owner asked whether Block Turn and Arrow Knots needed a Data safety fix. That day both public store pages listed four shared data types, but the collected section said no data was collected, while our site policies already listed Firebase Analytics and Crashlytics (861c5a3 on October 8, e13de33 on October 11).

The agent lacked console permission, so the owner entered the answers from a table, and at 14:58 the agent expanded the console preview and compared it line by line. On October 4, Play policy URLs couldn't be changed through the API either, so the owner changed four by hand. No app had the URL hard-coded, so that finished the move.

Nowhere to run a check between the four apps' code and a table

AXFamily goes further. Its processors live in processors.json, the policy tables are generated, and the CI job legal-consistency compares the output with the JSON and the in-app notice with the function constants. Its policy wasn't public on October 4, so we never compared the two side by side. We would still run the per-app audit on the four old apps.

Generating the tables alone could sit in the pre-commit check of the site repository, where the policies live. The hard part is comparing the JSON with app code: the four app repositories have no CI to run that, and SugarAlbum has had no commit since January 3. A per-app audit must be redone when the code changes; generation fails a check on a mismatch but needs somewhere in the app repository to run it. So far only AXFamily uses generation. Start any new app with it.

What reading the source cannot tell you, and legal review

  • Code can't confirm legal requirements. The section list came from a list of Korean privacy law items the agent wrote into its own prompt (purpose, items, retention, third parties and overseas transfers, destruction, user rights, privacy officer) plus earlier policies, and we have no record of a person or professional reviewing that list. The photo app policy the writers took their structure from on October 4 had no section on processors and overseas transfers; they added one, and the original got its own 18 days later in 45c6b76.
  • Source is not the shipped build. The SugarCard reviewer noted it could not tell which build was on Play, and SugarAlbum's merged manifest came from a January 3 build.
  • What an SDK collects isn't in the app's code. Commit ba0fe57 (September 23) added IP address, ad interactions and diagnostics to our first policy after the fact.

We haven't tried this on a service with many servers and processors, or on an outsourced app without source. We've only run it on Android apps with one operator.

Start by marking the negative claims in your published policy

If a sentence can't be tied to a line of code or a console value, delete it. Doubt "we don't collect" first.

  1. Fetch the published policy, split it into sentences and mark every negative claim.
  2. Note the app version and build a data flow table: data, destination, code location.
  3. Count permissions from the merged release manifest, not the source manifest. Permissions added by libraries appear only there.
  4. Read each SDK vendor's data disclosure and copy the items over.
  5. Read retention and deletion settings from the API instead of assuming them.
  6. Give the table and policy to a different agent or person and ask only for counter-evidence.
  7. Compare your Play Data safety answers with the same table.
  8. Record the audited commit next to the policy and run the diff below at each release. We have not done this step for the four apps.
# Permissions and dependencies changed since the audit
git diff $AUDITED HEAD -- '*AndroidManifest.xml' '*build.gradle*' '*libs.versions.toml'

# Permissions in the merged release manifest (path varies with AGP version)
grep -rh 'uses-permission' app/build/intermediates/merged_manifest/release/

# Firestore TTL and log retention
TOKEN=$(gcloud auth application-default print-access-token)
curl -s -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "x-goog-user-project: $PROJECT" \
  "https://firestore.googleapis.com/v1/projects/$PROJECT/databases/(default)/collectionGroups/$GROUP/fields/$FIELD" \
  | grep -A3 ttlConfig
curl -s -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "x-goog-user-project: $PROJECT" \
  "https://logging.googleapis.com/v2/projects/$PROJECT/locations/global/buckets/_Default" \
  | grep retentionDays

This diff does not see server code or request fields. SugarSpeller's repeated AI requests live under functions/, so these paths miss them. Add your server and network code paths.

Step 6 used a review prompt along these lines, shortened and with file paths removed.

Adversarially review the privacy policy pages against the real app source.
Read-only on the source repo. Check: every claim matches code (especially any
"we don't collect/send X" claims: look for counter-evidence: ad SDK, Firebase,
network calls, other paths), ko/en say the same thing.
FIX problems directly in those two files only.
Report: what you fixed, what remains uncertain.

The gate in our release gate and R8 crash note confirms the policy URL returns 200, which proves a page exists and says nothing about its sentences. The Firestore and logging calls copy the form the reviewer used on October 4; we did not rerun them for this note.

Four policies still dated October 4

All four policies still carry an effective date of October 4. In the repositories, SugarSpeller has reached 1.6.033, SugarCard 1.0.003 and SugarClip 1.2.002. A diff of SugarSpeller shows no new dependency in build.gradle, a manifest with queries for 3 sibling apps and one new game screen, and a server that can now send the same AI request up to 3 times. We haven't done the re-audit that decides whether any of this changes the policy.

The Play Data safety forms of those four apps have not finished being compared with these policies.

FAQ

Is a privacy policy template risky for an app?

It is when its sentences differ from the code. Ours said "not collected by me" while the app sent data to our server. Use a template as a table of contents and rewrite the sentences from code.

Can an AI agent write my privacy policy?

It can draft one, but don't publish that draft unreviewed. All four of our source-based drafts needed fixes from a second agent. Keep writer and reviewer separate, and decide on professional review separately.

What do I write when I can't verify something?

Delete the claim or state it broadly on the side of disclosure. We deleted unsupported numbers such as "integrity tokens 12 hours", and kept a permission we could not tie to a build as "may be declared". Don't write a denial like "we don't store this" until you've checked.
