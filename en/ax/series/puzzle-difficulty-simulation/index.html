Tuning puzzle difficulty by simulation: five cases

Built to its spec, our block puzzle could not be lost: a planning bot played 80 games and all 80 hit the 3,000-move cap. Five parts on fixing difficulty with bots, generators and player models.

5 parts

What this series found

  • Block Turn's spec promised that all three pieces in a tray could always be placed. Under that rule a planning bot played 80 games and all 80 reached the 3,000-move cap. We compared seven supply-rule versions with two bots, chose v7, and now keep the promise for the first 10 trays only. (Part 1: simulation bots)
  • All 1,100 Arrow Knots levels come from a reverse-construction generator built on a move rule fixed first, so every board is solvable as built. One generator fix cut the share of arrows removable on the first move in the hardest band from 0.50 to 0.11. (Part 2: the level generator)
  • Testers said the timer was too generous. A 158-level simulation set a 30% cut; the first proposal, 45%, left casual players a 1% three-star rate past level 500. (Part 3: time limits)
  • Seven of 16 ad-acquired devices stalled on board 4 without a single solve. Solving the first 10 boards exactly with a player model found four walls, but board 4 was not one of them, so the data could not say why those seven left. (Part 4: first-chapter walls)
  • We slowed a spelling mini game twice. Replaying the first slowdown in a player model, beginners still lost a heart in the first minute in 100% of runs: the block was recalling the right word, not speed. (Part 5: two speed fixes that failed)

This series is for solo developers and small teams making puzzle games. If you plan to generate levels, start with Part 2; if your game is live and day-one churn is high, start with Part 4. Parts 1 and 3 use the same idea, re-scoring one set of bot runs or play logs against each candidate rule, so they read well together. Part 5 is the failure case where simulation came only after complaints; read it the day someone tells you the game is too hard.

The order we now follow before changing a difficulty number

Each part ends with its own steps. Read side by side, they describe one routine. Each step below is in the list because skipping it cost us something in that part.

  1. Write the target before the first run: a three-star range per level band, or a test assertion such as "at most 10% of beginner runs lose a heart in the first minute" (Parts 3 and 5). In Part 5 the constants were picked first; if that happens, say so in the commit message.
  2. Let the simulator read its numbers from the shipped code or the exact build the player had, not a copy. Part 3's simulator reads its constants straight from the app's code; Part 5 copied the complained-about engine file into the test folder (Parts 3 and 5).
  3. Model a person who can fail, not one who knows every answer. Part 5's first test was a typist who knew every word, so slowing the fall looked like enough; Part 4's line-first model found walls that random win rates and tier labels did not.
  4. Use at least two player types. A greedy bot against a planning bot exposed the unlosable rule in Part 1; in Part 3 the first proposal, a 45% cut, left casual players a 1% three-star rate past level 500.
  5. Run once, store every run, and rescore the stored runs under each candidate rule instead of re-simulating per guess (Parts 1 and 3). Hand the person deciding two or three options with their later-band numbers.
  6. Check the result against real players before trusting it. Part 4 lined up 16 devices minute by minute and found the model's walls did not explain board 4; Part 5's players averaged level 2.0, below the model's 3.
  7. Pin the decision in tests that fail if the guard is removed: the planner never stopping under the old rule (Part 1), the pack gate recomputing metrics from the boards (Part 2), checkpoint tests for the new par (Part 3).

All five cases come from our own games, and the samples are small: 80 bot games, 158 simulated levels, 16 ad-acquired devices. The routine carries over to other puzzles; the thresholds do not.
